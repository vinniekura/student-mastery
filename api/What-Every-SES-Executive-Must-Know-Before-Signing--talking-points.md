## Slide 4: The Data Rights Trap — What Happens to Your Data Inside the Platform?

**Setup:**
Here's a scenario I want you to imagine. Your agency has procured an AI platform to assist with policy drafting. Your team starts using it. Briefings go in. Cabinet-sensitive deliberations go in. Citizen records go in. Now — where does that data go from there?

**Key Point:**
The uncomfortable answer, in many AI vendor contracts, is that it goes into their model. Many platforms — particularly those built on large language models — contain training clauses that allow the vendor to use data submitted through the platform to improve their underlying AI. In a commercial context, that's an inconvenience. In government, it's a serious problem. Your agency's data could be shaping a model that's being sold to other organisations, including potentially your counterparts in other jurisdictions or the private sector.

**Bridge:**
The good news is this is a solvable problem — but only if you know what questions to ask before you sign.

## Slide 5: Three Data Rights Questions Every Contract Must Answer

**Setup:**
Before any AI vendor contract goes to signature, your legal and data teams should be able to answer three specific questions. If they can't — in plain language, in writing — the contract is not ready to sign. Full stop.

**Key Point:**
Question one: can the vendor use your data to train or improve their model? That should be explicitly prohibited for government data, and the prohibition needs to appear in the contract itself — not in a terms-of-service document the vendor can update without your knowledge. Question two: where is your data stored and processed? If the answer is "global infrastructure," push back hard — that's a deflection, not an answer, and it has real implications for data sovereignty under the APS AI Plan. Question three: what happens to your data when the contract ends? You need a clear, enforceable deletion clause with a defined timeframe, not a vague gesture toward industry best practice.

**Bridge:**
Data rights are about what the vendor can do with your information. Lock-in is about what you can do when you want to leave — and that's where the next trap sits.

---

## Slide 6: Lock-In Is a Procurement Risk, Not Just a Technical One

**Setup:**
When executives hear "vendor lock-in," they often assume it's a problem for their technology team to manage. It's a developer issue, an architecture issue, a migration issue. I want to reframe that for you today.

**Key Point:**
Lock-in is a procurement risk. It has budget implications, continuity implications, and sovereignty implications that sit squarely in your remit as an SES officer. When your agency becomes dependent on a single vendor's proprietary AI system — their data formats, their APIs, their pricing structure — you lose negotiating leverage. You lose the ability to respond to better solutions in the market. And in a worst-case scenario, you lose the ability to exit the relationship without significant disruption to service delivery. That is an executive-level problem, not a technical one.

**Bridge:**
The question is what you can actually do about it at the contract stage — and there are concrete terms you should be demanding.

## Slide 7: Negotiating Against Lock-In — What to Demand in Writing

**Setup:**
The good news about lock-in is that it's largely preventable if you address it before you sign. The challenge is that most vendors won't volunteer these protections — you have to ask for them explicitly, and you have to know what to ask for.

**Key Point:**
There are three things you should demand in writing. First, data portability — your agency must be able to export its data in a usable, non-proprietary format at any time, not just at contract termination. Second, API and integration standards — where possible, insist on open standards that don't tie your systems exclusively to that vendor's ecosystem. Third, exit assistance provisions — the contract should require the vendor to actively support your transition if you choose to move to a different platform, including a reasonable transition period and documented handover requirements. These aren't unreasonable asks. They're standard practice in mature technology procurement, and any reputable vendor should be willing to commit to them.

**Bridge:**
You've protected your data. You've built in an exit. Now let's talk about what happens when the AI makes a mistake — because it will — and who is accountable.

## Slide 8: The Liability Gap — Who Is Accountable When AI Gets It Wrong?

**Setup:**
AI systems make errors. That's not a criticism of the technology — it's a characteristic of it. The question is not whether errors will occur. The question is what happens when they do, and who bears the consequences.

**Key Point:**
In most AI vendor contracts, the answer to that question is structured in the vendor's favour. Liability caps are set low. Indemnity clauses exclude consequential damages. Warranties are limited to the system performing "substantially as described" — which is a standard that gives vendors enormous room to move. For a government agency, the stakes are different. An AI error that affects a citizen's benefit assessment, a procurement decision, or a policy recommendation isn't just a commercial inconvenience — it can have serious legal, ethical, and reputational consequences. Your contract needs to reflect that asymmetry.

**Bridge:**
Let's look specifically at what to watch for in the liability clauses, because the details matter enormously here.

## Slide 9: Understanding Liability in AI Vendor Agreements

**Setup:**
Liability language in AI contracts tends to be dense and strategically vague. Vendors rely on the fact that most people reviewing these agreements don't have time to interrogate every clause. Your job is to make sure someone on your team does.

**Key Point:**
There are three liability structures you need to understand. First, liability caps — most vendors will cap their financial liability at the value of the contract, or sometimes less. For a significant AI deployment, that cap may be completely inadequate relative to the harm an error could cause. Second, exclusion of consequential damages — this is where vendors exclude liability for downstream impacts, which is precisely where government harm tends to manifest. Third, fit-for-purpose warranties — push for explicit language that the system is fit for the specific government use case you're deploying it for, not just for general commercial use. These are negotiable. But you have to ask.

**Bridge:**
There's one more requirement that's now coming directly from the APS AI Plan 2025 itself, and it changes what you're obligated to include in any AI vendor agreement.

## Slide 10: The Transparency Clause Requirement Under APS AI Plan 2025

**Setup:**
The APS AI Plan 2025 isn't just a strategic document — it creates specific obligations that flow into your procurement decisions. One of the most important for AI vendor contracts is the transparency requirement, and it has direct implications for what your agreements must include.

**Key Point:**
Under the framework, agencies are expected to maintain visibility over how AI systems are making decisions — particularly where those decisions affect citizens or public outcomes. That means your vendor contracts need to include transparency clauses that require the vendor to provide meaningful documentation of how the system works, what data it uses, how it handles edge cases, and how it flags uncertainty or low-confidence outputs. A vendor who won't commit to that level of transparency in writing is a vendor who is telling you something important about how they view accountability. And as the Chief AI Officer mandate takes hold, your agency's ability to demonstrate compliance will depend on having these clauses in place before you need them.

## Slide 11: Your Pre-Signature Checklist — Five Executive Decisions

**Setup:**
I want to give you something you can use on Monday morning. Not a process framework. Not a governance matrix. Five specific decisions that you, as the accountable executive, need to make before your name goes on an AI vendor agreement.

**Key Point:**
One — confirm that a qualified legal and data team has produced a plain-language summary of the data rights the vendor is claiming. If that summary doesn't exist, the contract isn't ready. Two — verify that data residency is explicitly defined in the contract, not delegated to a terms-of-service document. Three — check that lock-in protections — portability, open standards, exit assistance — are written into the agreement. Four — review the liability caps and consequential damages exclusions against the realistic scale of harm your use case could produce. Five — confirm that transparency clauses meet your obligations under the APS AI Plan 2025. Each of these is a yes-or-no question. If any answer is no, you have work to do before you sign.

**Bridge:**

## Slide 12: Building the Right Team for AI Contract Review

**Setup:**
One of the patterns I see repeatedly in government AI procurement is that contract review is treated as a legal function alone. Legal reviews the agreement, flags the obvious issues, and the contract moves forward. That approach is not sufficient for AI vendor agreements, and I want to explain why.

**Key Point:**
AI contracts sit at the intersection of technology, law, data governance, and policy — and you need expertise from all four disciplines in the room at the same time. Your legal team can identify the liability exposure. Your data team can assess whether the data rights clauses match your actual data handling obligations. Your technology team can evaluate whether the lock-in provisions are meaningful in practice. And your policy team can assess whether the transparency commitments will hold up against your accountability obligations to Ministers and the public. The executive's role in this process is not to understand every technical detail — it's to assemble the right team, ask the right questions, and not sign until you have answers you can stand behind.
